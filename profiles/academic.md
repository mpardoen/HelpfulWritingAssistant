# Profile: academic

For theses, dissertations, monographs and long research reports: any work that builds an argument across chapters.

## Chapter record

`record.md` is the only thing later consistency checks will know about this chapter, so it records exactly what the chapter commits the author to. Sections:

- **Purpose**: what the chapter sets out to do and how it fits in the whole, in two or three lines.
- **Claims**: each claim the chapter makes, one per bullet, with its strength as written (shown, argued, suggested, assumed) and the paragraph (`¶n`).
- **Definitions and terminology**: every term defined or used in a technical sense, with the definition as given. Note abbreviations and their expansion.
- **Notation**: symbols, variables and units introduced, and what each denotes.
- **Research questions, hypotheses, contributions**: as stated in this chapter, verbatim where possible.
- **Methods and data**: what was done, on what; every number that could be repeated elsewhere (sample sizes, dates, parameters, thresholds).
- **Results**: findings with their figures, and the tables or figures that carry them.
- **Forward and backward references**: what the chapter promises that later chapters will do, and what it relies on from earlier ones.
- **Key sources**: the works the chapter leans on most, and what it takes from each.
- **Limitations acknowledged**

## Consistency

Compare the chapter against the earlier records for:

- terminology: a term used with a different meaning than its earlier definition, or two terms for the same thing;
- notation: a symbol reused for something else, units that change;
- numbers: sample sizes, dates, parameters and results that differ from what was stated before;
- claims: a claim that contradicts, weakens or overstates an earlier one without saying so;
- research questions and contributions: whether what this chapter does matches what earlier chapters announced, and whether earlier promises addressed to this chapter are kept;
- cross-references that point to something that the earlier records do not contain;
- a source cited for a position different from the one it was cited for earlier.

## Style

- clarity: sentences that must be read twice, undefined jargon, ambiguous pronouns;
- argument structure: whether each section states its point, whether steps are missing, whether conclusions follow from what precedes them;
- strength of wording against strength of evidence: overclaiming, and hedging stacked so deep that nothing is asserted;
- signposting: too little to follow, or so much that it replaces content;
- register and consistency of person and tense;
- paragraphs that carry several ideas, or none;
- repetition between sections.

Do not check citation formatting against a style guide unless `BOOK.md` names one.

## Reader review

Written in the reader's own voice and from their own level of expertise. Sections:

- **In one breath**: what they understood the chapter to establish, two or three sentences. If this differs from what the author intended, that is the finding.
- **What convinced me**
- **Where I was lost or unconvinced**: with the paragraph, and what would have helped.
- **Questions I would ask the author**: as they would at a defence or in a review.
- **What I expect from the next chapters**
- **Verdict**: accept as is / minor revisions / major revisions / not convinced, and why.

## Reader memory

What this reader carries away from the chapter, in their own words. Sections:

- **What I understood the argument to be**: as retained by this reader. A reader from outside the field retains less and may have it partly wrong; that is part of the simulation.
- **Terms and ideas I now take for granted**
- **What I am convinced of**
- **My doubts and objections**
- **What I am waiting for**: promises the author made that this reader intends to hold them to.

## Whole-work analysis

- **The argument as a whole**: the thesis in a paragraph, as the text actually supports it, and whether that matches the stated one.
- **Research questions and contributions**: for each one announced, where it is answered and how well.
- **Chapter roles**: what each chapter contributes, overlaps between them, and anything missing.
- **Consistency across chapters**: terminology, notation, numbers and claims that differ, with both locations.
- **Evidence and method**: where the evidence is thinnest relative to the claim it supports.
- **Promises**: forward references never honoured, and results never announced.
- **Readability**: where a reader is most likely to give up.
- **Likely examiner questions**: the ten hardest ones.
- **Revision priorities**: an ordered list, most important first.

## Typical readers

The supervisor, the external examiner from a neighbouring field, the methodologist, the specialist who knows the literature better than the author, the intelligent reader from outside the discipline.
